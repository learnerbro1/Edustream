Light — Reflection and Refraction

CBSE Class 10 • Physics — Chapter 1 • Quick Revision Sheet + PYQs

1. Reflection of Light

Reflection is the bouncing back of light when it strikes a polished surface.

Laws of Reflection

  1. The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.
  2. The angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection (∠i = ∠r).

These laws hold true for all reflecting surfaces — plane as well as spherical.

Spherical Mirrors

Concave mirror (converging) — reflecting surface curves inward, like the inside of a spoon. Used in torches, headlights, shaving mirrors, solar cookers.
Convex mirror (diverging) — reflecting surface bulges outward. Used as vehicle rear-view mirrors (wider field of view, always gives an erect, diminished image).

Key terms

  • Pole (P) — centre of the mirror's reflecting surface.
  • Centre of curvature (C) — centre of the sphere of which the mirror is a part.
  • Radius of curvature (R) — distance from P to C.
  • Principal focus (F) — point on the axis where parallel rays converge (or appear to diverge from).
  • Focal length (f) — distance from P to F. Relation: R = 2f
Mirror formula:
1/v + 1/u = 1/f
Magnification:
m = h′/h = −v/u

New Cartesian sign convention

  • All distances measured from the pole.
  • Distances in the direction of incident light (i.e. to the right) are positive; against it (to the left) are negative.
  • Heights measured upward are positive; downward are negative.

2. Refraction of Light

Refraction is the bending of light as it passes from one transparent medium to another, caused by a change in speed.

Laws of Refraction

  1. The incident ray, the refracted ray, and the normal all lie in the same plane.
  2. Snell's law: the ratio sin i / sin r is constant for a given pair of media.
Refractive index:
n = speed of light in vacuum / speed of light in medium
n21 = sin i / sin r

A ray of light travelling from a rarer to a denser medium bends towards the normal; a ray going from denser to rarer bends away from the normal. A ray incident normally (along the normal) passes through undeviated.

Refraction through a rectangular glass slab

The emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray but laterally displaced, because the bending at the first surface (rarer→denser) is exactly cancelled by opposite bending at the second surface (denser→rarer).

Spherical Lenses

Convex lens (converging) — thicker at the middle. Real focus in front of the lens (on the far side from the object).
Concave lens (diverging) — thinner at the middle. Virtual focus, on the same side as the object.
Lens formula:
1/v − 1/u = 1/f
Magnification:
m = h′/h = v/u
Power of a lens:
P = 1/f (f in metres); unit = dioptre (D)

Power of a convex lens is positive; power of a concave lens is negative. 1 D = power of a lens of focal length 1 m.

★ Quick memory tips

  • Concave mirror = "SALT" — behaves like a converging system, focus is real.
  • For mirrors: use the mirror formula sign convention (distances measured from pole, incident-ray direction positive). For lenses: distances measured from optical centre.
  • Remember: Real image → always inverted; Virtual image → always erect, for both mirrors and lenses.
  • n21 × n12 = 1, and n21 = n2/n1 (absolute refractive indices).
