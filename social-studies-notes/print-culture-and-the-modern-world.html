Print Culture and the Modern World

CBSE Class 10 • History — Chapter 5 • Quick Revision Sheet + PYQs

1. The First Printed Books

The earliest kind of print technology was developed in China, Japan, and Korea. It was a system of hand printing.

  • China: Books were printed by rubbing paper against the inked surface of woodblocks. The traditional Chinese 'accordion book' was folded and stitched at the side.
  • Japan: Buddhist missionaries from China introduced hand-printing technology into Japan. The oldest Japanese book is the Buddhist Diamond Sutra (printed 868 AD).

2. Print Comes to Europe

Marco Polo brought woodblock printing technology to Italy from China. However, the breakthrough occurred in Germany.

Gutenberg and the Printing Press:

Johann Gutenberg developed the first known printing press in the 1430s. The first book he printed was the Bible. This shifted Europe from a hearing public to a reading public.

Fig 5.1: The Gutenberg Printing Press, a revolution in written communication

3. The Print Revolution and Its Impact

Print created a new culture of reading and opened up new ways of looking at things.

  • Religious Debates: Martin Luther wrote Ninety-Five Theses criticizing the practices of the Catholic Church. His printed writings led to the Protestant Reformation. He said, "Printing is the ultimate gift of God and the greatest one."
  • The French Revolution: Print popularized the ideas of Enlightenment thinkers (Voltaire, Rousseau), exposing the absurdity of the monarchical system and preparing minds for rebellion.

4. India and the World of Print

India had a rich tradition of handwritten manuscripts in Sanskrit, Arabic, and Persian, but they were fragile and expensive to produce.

Print Arrives: The Portuguese introduced the printing press to Goa. Catholic priests printed the first Tamil and Konkani books.
Religious Reform: Rammohun Roy published the Sambad Kaumudi. Conservative Hindus opposed it with the Samachar Chandrika.

5. Print and Censorship in India

After the Revolt of 1857, the attitude to freedom of the press changed. The British government wanted to clamp down on the "native" press.

Vernacular Press Act (1878): Modeled on the Irish Press Laws, it provided the government with extensive rights to censor reports and editorials in the vernacular press.
